The Essential 12

Scuba Equipment

Each piece of equipment serves a precise purpose. Understanding your gear is the first step to becoming a confident, safe diver.

01
Dive Mask

Creates an air space in front of your eyes so you can see clearly underwater. Without it, water refraction makes everything blurry. Tempered glass lenses resist pressure at depth. A good seal against your face is everything.

First purchase — fit matters most
02
Regulator

The heart of your kit. Connects to your tank and reduces high-pressure air (~200 bar) down to a breathable pressure on demand. The first stage sits on the tank valve; the second stage goes in your mouth and delivers air only when you inhale.

Most critical piece of equipment
03
BCD — Buoyancy Control Device

An inflatable jacket that lets you rise, sink, or hover neutrally at any depth. Inflate to go up, deflate to go down. Mastering your BCD is what makes you look effortless underwater. It also holds your tank securely on your back.

Controls your position in the water
04
Tank (Cylinder)

Stores your compressed breathing gas — typically air — at up to 300 bar. Tanks are aluminium or steel, usually 10–12 litres capacity. The tank valve connects to your regulator first stage. Most dive shops fill and rent them for you.

Usually rented from dive shops
05
Wetsuit

Traps a thin layer of water against your skin which your body heats up, keeping you insulated. Neoprene thickness ranges from 1mm (tropics) to 7mm (cold water). A drysuit seals you completely dry and is used in frigid conditions.

3mm suits suit Australian waters
06
Fins

Transfer your leg power into efficient forward movement. Full-foot fins slip on like shoes; open-heel fins are adjustable and worn with dive boots. Never use your arms to swim underwater — let your fins do all the work and save your energy.

Second essential purchase
07
Weight System

Neoprene suits and your lungs make you float. Lead weights counteract this buoyancy so you can descend and stay neutrally buoyant. Worn on a belt or in BCD pockets. Quick-release buckles are a critical safety feature — you must be able to ditch weights fast.

Amount varies by wetsuit thickness
08
Dive Computer

Tracks your depth and elapsed time, continuously calculating how long you can safely stay at each depth to avoid decompression sickness. Worn on your wrist like a watch. Modern computers are beginner-friendly and replace complex manual dive tables entirely.

Every diver should own one
09
Surface Marker Buoy (SMB)

An inflatable orange tube you send to the surface at the end of a dive to signal your position to your boat. Without one, boats cannot see you bobbing in open water. Inflated at depth via a small spool and reel — a vital piece of safety kit.

Non-negotiable in open water
10
Dive Light / Torch

Restores true colour below about 10 metres — red wavelengths are absorbed first, making everything appear blue-green. Essential for night dives, cavern exploration, and illuminating crevices where marine life hides. Even on bright daytime dives, a torch reveals hidden creatures.

Brings colour back to the deep
11
Snorkel

Lets you breathe at the surface without spending any tank air — useful while waiting on the surface, swimming out to a dive site, or returning to a boat. A simple J-tube design is the most reliable; fancy purge valves are more parts to break.

Conserves tank air at the surface
12
Hood & Gloves

Your head loses a significant amount of body heat underwater — a neoprene hood plugs that gap. Gloves protect your hands from cold and accidental reef scrapes. Many dive sites actually discourage gloves to prevent divers from touching delicate coral.

Recommended below 18°C

Stay Alive Down There

Golden Safety Rules

Scuba diving is statistically very safe — when you follow these non-negotiable principles.

💨
Never hold your breath

Breathe continuously, always. Holding your breath while ascending can cause a lung overexpansion injury — the most serious injury in recreational diving.

⬆️
Ascend slowly

Rise no faster than 9–18 metres per minute. Ascending too quickly allows dissolved nitrogen to form bubbles in your blood — decompression sickness (the bends).

🤝
Always dive with a buddy

Your buddy is your first line of emergency response. Stay within arm's reach. Solo diving is an advanced specialty — never do it uncertified.

📋
Plan the dive, dive the plan

Agree on max depth, turnaround air pressure, and ascent route before you enter the water. In-water hand signals are not the time to negotiate the plan.

🔍
Do a buddy check (BWRAF)

BCD, Weights, Releases, Air, Final OK — every single dive without exception. Even divers with 1,000 logged dives run this check before every entry.

✈️
Don't fly after diving

Wait at least 12–24 hours after your last dive before boarding a plane. The reduced cabin pressure can trigger decompression sickness even after a surface interval.

Just Starting Out?

Six Things New
Divers Wish They Knew

Every expert was once a beginner. These are the insights that make the biggest difference in your first year of diving.

1
Get certified first — no shortcuts

A PADI, SSI, or GUE Open Water course takes about 3–4 days and covers everything in a structured, safe environment. It's the foundation that every other skill is built on.

2
Slow your breathing right down

Long, slow, deep breaths from your diaphragm conserve tank air dramatically and keep your heart rate calm. An anxious diver doing short fast breaths can empty a tank in half the time of a relaxed one.

3
Equalise your ears early and often

Pinch your nose and blow gently every metre during descent. Don't wait until your ears hurt — that means the pressure has already built up too much and you risk injury.

4
Hands off — touch absolutely nothing

Coral takes decades to grow and one touch can kill it. Many reef animals sting, bite, or are venomous. Keep your hands clasped, your fins off the reef, and hover slightly above everything.

5
Rent before you commit to buying

Try different equipment through rental before spending thousands on your own kit. Your two best early purchases are a well-fitting mask (fit is personal) and your own fins.

6
Log every single dive

Record your depth, bottom time, water temperature, visibility, and what you saw after every dive. Advanced certifications require a minimum number of logged dives, and it's wonderful to look back on years later.
